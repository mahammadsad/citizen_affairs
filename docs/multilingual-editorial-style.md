# Citizen Affairs multilingual editorial style

This guide applies to public English, Bengali and Hindi articles. The goal is to help readers understand the verified update and decide what to do next without clickbait, repetition or machine-translated phrasing.

## Shared article structure

1. **Headline:** authority or subject + verified action/status + practical outcome.
2. **Description:** one or two sentences explaining who is affected and why the update matters.
3. **Key points:** two to five short facts that answer the reader's immediate questions.
4. **Opening paragraph:** state the confirmed update, date or scope before background information.
5. **Body:** use descriptive H2 sections, short paragraphs and lists only where they improve scanning.
6. **Official sources:** distinguish a source statement from Citizen Affairs explanation and clearly state uncertainty.

## Headline rules

- Put the organisation, scheme, examination or service near the beginning.
- Include the result, deadline, benefit, vacancy count or required action when it is confirmed.
- Do not hide the main fact to manufacture curiosity.
- Avoid repeated punctuation, unsupported superlatives and television-style urgency.
- Do not write a headline as a complete copy of the first paragraph.

Recommended pattern:

> Responsible authority or subject + main update + practical outcome

Examples:

- **English:** SSC releases examination schedule; check subject-wise dates
- **Bengali:** SSC পরীক্ষার সূচি প্রকাশ করেছে, বিষয়ভিত্তিক তারিখ দেখুন
- **Hindi:** SSC ने परीक्षा कार्यक्रम जारी किया, विषयवार तारीखें देखें

## English

- Prefer direct, plain Indian English.
- Keep sentences mostly under 25 words.
- Use active voice when the responsible authority is known.
- Explain administrative abbreviations on first use.
- Use sentence case for headlines and section headings.

## Bengali

- Use natural standard Bengali, not word-for-word English syntax.
- Prefer familiar public-service terms; retain an English official name when translating it could create confusion.
- Give Bengali and Devanagari scripts adequate line space; do not use artificial letter spacing.
- Keep long compound sentences to a minimum.
- Place dates, amounts and eligibility conditions close to the action they qualify.

## Hindi

- Use standard, accessible Hindi rather than highly Sanskritised or television-style language.
- Keep recognised official English names where readers are likely to search for them.
- Avoid translating the same administrative term differently within one article.
- Use short sentences and clearly identify the authority responsible for a decision.

## Paragraph and section rules

- A public article needs an introductory prose paragraph before its first H2.
- Long articles need at least two descriptive H2 sections.
- One paragraph should normally cover one idea.
- Break a paragraph before it becomes difficult to scan on a phone.
- Do not use an H1 in the article body; the page template provides it.
- Do not begin the hierarchy with H3.

## Key-point rules

- Provide two to five items.
- Each item should answer a different practical question.
- Keep each item under 260 characters.
- Do not repeat the headline in bullet form.
- Do not make eligibility, approval or outcome guarantees that the official source does not make.

## Verification language

Use precise boundaries:

- “The notice states…” for a source claim.
- “Citizen Affairs could not independently confirm…” where verification is incomplete.
- “Submission does not guarantee approval” when an authority retains discretion.
- “Check the current official notice before acting” when deadlines, fees or availability can change.

Never label content “officially confirmed” unless it satisfies the independent fact-checking and primary-source requirements enforced by the content validator.
